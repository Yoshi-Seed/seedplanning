Daily Life Challenges

Invisible Symptoms
and Fluctuations

The symptoms of myasthenia gravis are hard to see from the outside, and some patients found it difficult to convey their condition to those around them. Symptom fluctuations — worsening in the evening, varying greatly from day to day — require flexible adjustments to work, household tasks, and social plans.

Personal Coping Strategies

Patients faced specific challenges: reduced walking distance, difficulty with stairs and fine motor tasks, and inability to keep their arms raised. While some felt resistance to using canes or assistive devices, they accepted them to conserve energy. In daily life, they reduce their burden through creative solutions — adapting cooking tools and household items, doing tasks earlier in the day, and choosing not to overexert themselves.

Emotional Burden

On the other hand, patients shared that being misunderstood or receiving insensitive remarks — because their condition is not visible — has been a significant emotional burden.

Patient Voices

Currently, symptoms worsen from the evening onward, with heaviness in arms and legs forcing her to lie down on many days. Side effects from injection treatment sometimes require sudden changes to plans, and she feels daily life always requires adjustment. She does housework during hours when she feels well, and relies on family in the evenings, trying not to push herself.

Patient A (Female, 20s)

Double vision, reduced motor function, and high blood sugar have made movement and daily activities a major burden. He currently spends more than half the day lying down, with limited activity levels. To conserve energy, he consciously decides what not to do, and lives using assistive devices and an electric wheelchair.

Patient B (Male, 50s)

Currently, medication effects fluctuate, and by evening she can't raise her arms, making housework difficult — she needs to organize her entire life around an earlier schedule. Because her condition is not visible, she has been hurt by comments like "you're so young," and insensitive remarks about physical changes from treatment.

Patient C (Female, 30s)

She still experiences double vision, and feels anxious about fine motor tasks and moving through uneven areas. She sometimes cuts her fingers while trimming nails or cooking, and when going out, she navigates using the range of vision from one eye. Because it's hard for others to see her symptoms, even wearing a help mark doesn't always lead to accommodation…

Patient D (Female, 40s)

She continues to experience hand heaviness and fatigue, making tasks like using a hair dryer or writing burdensome. Some days she feels short of breath, and she does housework in intervals, resting as needed. Because her condition isn't visible, she's often told "it's a curable disease, right?" and finds it difficult to explain her illness in a way others understand.

Patient E (Female, 30s)

Her walking distance has decreased significantly, and going out alone has become difficult. Symptoms like drooping eyelids, double vision, and shortness of breath are hard for others to see, making it difficult to convey her condition. She has come to accept the use of assistive tools and choosing not to push herself.

Patient F (Female, 30s)

To avoid fatigue, he avoids stairs as much as possible, carries a cane for long outings, and adjusts housework routines to avoid keeping his arms raised. He makes a conscious effort to conserve energy in daily life. Because the disease is not visible, he has experienced hurtful comments even while wearing a help mark…
